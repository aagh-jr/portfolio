Overview

SkinSavior is a skincare app my co-founder and I are building to answer a question the existing tools do not answer well: not whether an ingredient is safe, but how strong the evidence is behind what a product says it does. Every app in this space scores safety. We decided early to compete on evidence quality instead, and most of the product decisions since have come back to that 1 choice.

I own product strategy, the app experience, the skin quiz, the evidence-grading engine, the catalog pipeline and the AI integrations. My co-founder owns the marketing site, database organization and the user survey. It is a working cross-platform prototype (web and mobile), built with Claude Code as the main coding assistant. It is pre-launch, so this is a story about decisions and shipped scope, not traction. I would rather show the reasoning than invent a number.

Problem

Skincare marketing runs on claims. “Clinically proven,” “reduces fine lines in 4 weeks,” “dermatologist recommended.” A shopper in the aisle has almost no way to check any of it. The tools that try to help — Yuka, EWG Skin Deep, INCIDecoder, SkinSort, Paula's Choice — turn a product's ingredient list into a single safety score or a list of warnings. That answers “will this hurt me.” It does nothing for the question most people are actually asking, which is “will this do what it says.”

There is a second layer most people miss until they start building. Under FDA and FTC rules, acne, eczema and rosacea are disease classifications. The moment a product claims to treat one of them it is making a drug claim, no matter how the sentence is worded. So the obvious feature, “tell the user what will fix their acne,” is not a gap competitors overlooked. It is a legal line, and anything that touches “does this work” has to get it right first.

Goals

  • Give a non-ingredient person a plain-language answer to “which of this product's claims are actually supported,” per claim, without making them learn chemistry.
  • Ground every grade in a standard I did not invent, so the answer is defensible and not just another opinion score.
  • Stay on the cosmetic side of the FDA line in every piece of copy the product produces.
  • Ship the differentiator first and hold parity features until they can build on it.

Constraints

  • 2 people, no funding. Every API call has a real cost, so where LLMs run and how often is a product decision, not just an engineering one.
  • I am the only person on the product and design side, so scope discipline is most of the job.
  • Claims language has to stay compliant, which is why we wrote the policy down before building the features that depend on it.

Process

The work ran in 3 passes: figure out where the real gap was (discovery and research), decide what to build against it (and what to refuse), then build and ship the parts that stand on their own. The sections below follow that order.

Discovery / Research

Competitive analysis

My co-founder and I mapped the incumbents against what they actually ship. The finding that shaped strategy was not a gap. It was how full the parity space already is. SkinSort alone ships scanning, match scores, routine tracking, a dupe finder, incompatibility warnings and community. Building any of those first would have been months of work to arrive at “also does what SkinSort does.” The 1 thing none of them do is grade the evidence behind a specific claim.

Desk research

If the product is going to say “the evidence for this is strong,” it needs a standard I did not make up. I adapted 2 frameworks clinicians already use: GRADE for rating certainty of evidence, and the Oxford CEBM levels for ranking study types (systematic reviews above randomized trials above cohort studies). PubMed is the source because it is free, structured and citable.

User interview

1 interview so far, semi-structured, with a skincare enthusiast who researches every purchase, cares about affordability and repurchasability, and judges products by results. I am not going to call 1 conversation a sample. I am treating what she said as hypotheses for the next 5 or 6 interviews. It produced more usable findings than I expected, and several lined up with decisions I had already made, marked “informed” below.

The findings that moved the product:

  • She likes skincare but does not “do ingredients.” Asked to name what the main ingredient in each product in her routine does, she could not. A person who researches everything, and the ingredient layer is still opaque to her.
  • She reasons about mechanism anyway. She buys a serum marketed for oily skin to handle acne, because it targets the oil, which is the cause. The label category and her reason for buying were different things.
  • Affordability means repurchasability. A $20 cream 10 minutes away beats a $40 cream 30 minutes away, because she needs to buy it again. Small rotation, finishes a product before judging it, no repurchase if nothing improved.
  • She does not track her routine over time and would not use a diary. When she switches products the old one is “gone in her head.”
  • Marketing skepticism is already part of her process. She avoids ad-driven brands and reads Sephora's sponsored-review disclosures to decide how much to trust a review.
  • She dupes by purpose and texture, not ingredient overlap. If recommendations were built for her, she would rank them by product class, purpose and texture.

Design

Five decisions defined the product. For each I have written what I chose and what I rejected.

[IMAGE: Evidence-quality strategy]

1. Compete on evidence quality, not safety

Safety scoring is crowded and largely solved. Evidence grading is unoccupied, harder to build, and answers the question people are actually asking. This also fixed the build order: the grading engine first, and any feature that only makes sense once grading exists second.

[IMAGE: Per-claim evidence rows]

2. Grade per claim, not per ingredient

The first evidence meter gave 1 score per ingredient. I threw it out. Niacinamide has strong evidence for some outcomes and weak evidence for others, and a single number averages that into noise. The shipped design shows 1 row per claim, so a product's “brightening” claim and its “pore minimizing” claim get separate verdicts.

Informed by finding 6. Her frustration with “everything” products is what a per-ingredient average produces.

[IMAGE: Deterministic grading pipeline]

3. Deterministic grade, LLMs only at the edges

A grade a language model could hallucinate is not a grade. The pipeline runs in 3 stages with a wall between them. Stage 1, an LLM extracts structured fields from a study (design, sample size, outcome). Stage 2 is deterministic: the GRADE and CEBM rules produce a certainty level with no model in the loop. Stage 3 hands the finished grade to a cheap fast model to write plain-language copy. The model can phrase the result, it cannot change it. This also set the cost structure: expensive reasoning runs once per study at ingestion, the runtime step uses the cheapest model that writes acceptable prose.

Informed by finding 5. Her conflict-of-interest worry is exactly why the grade cannot be something a model decides.

[IMAGE: Skin profile quiz]

4. Profile skin on 4 axes, and match the model to the job

The quiz does not file you under oily, dry or combination. It profiles 4 independent axes (oiliness, pigmentation, aging, sensitivity), adds a phototype question and the safety fields a dermatology intake form uses (prescription retinoids, pregnancy, known reactions). Those safety fields are also the compliance guardrail: they let the product avoid unsafe ingredients without making a treatment claim. Separately, model usage splits by job: the rarely-run URL ingest uses Claude Opus 4.8 (real reasoning, has to parse a page), the constantly-run explainer uses Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite (just phrasing a finished grade).

Informed by finding 2. She reasons about the cause, not the label, and a single type bucket cannot represent that.

[IMAGE: Claims policy guardrail]

5. Write the claims policy first, and refuse the parity features

We wrote the claims-language policy (which terms are disease terms, how to phrase evidence without implying treatment) into the repo before building the evidence card or the explainer prompt, so every template was written against a rule instead of audited after. Match scoring, routine compatibility and a dupe finder are all on the roadmap and none are built, on purpose, because each gets better once it can consume a grade and is a parity feature until then. The interview also changed how I will build the dupe finder: I had it as ingredient overlap, she dupes by purpose and texture, so the ranking will be product class, purpose and texture first.

Informed by findings 3, 4 and 6. Prices are stored per product for repurchasability, and the skin diary I had scoped early is deprioritized.

Solution to the Problem

The product answers “which claims are supported” by turning each product page into a per-claim evidence view built on the deterministic pipeline above, fed by a real catalog and a quiz that personalizes without needing chemistry from the user. Everything in the next section is built and running in the live demo. The parts that are still roadmap are named as roadmap, not shown as shipped.

Learning & Reflection

Talk to 1 person sooner

I built for months on competitive analysis and desk research before I sat down with a single user. The strategy held up, but 1 hour with 1 person re-scoped a roadmap feature and gave me a sharper way to describe the problem than anything I had written. It would not have changed the core bet. It would have saved me from scoping a skin diary.

Scope the legal line first

Writing the claims policy before the evidence card felt slow. It was the cheapest decision in the project, because every prompt and template was written against it instead of rewritten after.

Deterministic core, LLM edges

Keeping the thing that has to be right (the grade) out of any model's hands, and using models only to extract inputs and phrase outputs, is a pattern I would reuse anywhere the output has to be trusted. It is also the cheapest architecture.

AI building changes what the job is

Claude Code let me ship a cross-platform product as the only person on product and design. It did not decide what to build. The hardest work was saying no to the 3 features every competitor already has. A practical note from the same stretch: I ran 2 coding agents against the same working tree during a push and had to start re-checking git state before every commit to keep them from clobbering each other.
